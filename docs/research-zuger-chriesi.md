# Zuger Chriesi — historical research

Background research for «Vom Chriesibaum ins Glas» (Stadtmagazin Zug, spring 2027, theme «Ausgegraben»).
Compiled 2026-10-20 from public web sources (listed at the end). **Confidence** marks how well each claim is sourced:
✅ documented in official or scholarly sources · ⚠️ only in tourism or association material, or the sources disagree · ❌ contradicted.

---

## 1. Short version

- **The cherry is older in Zug than the city.** A single wild-cherry stone was found at the Late Bronze Age lakeshore village of **Zug-Sumpf** (around 1060–880 BC). Cultivated sweet and sour cherry stones from the **Roman water mill at Cham-Hagendorn** are dated **AD 170–270**. ✅
- **Written records start in the mid-14th century.** Good sources only exist from the 17th and 18th centuries. ✅
- **Chriesimärt (cherry market):** first mentioned in **1627**. It is often called Switzerland's oldest cherry market. The date is ✅; the "oldest" claim is ⚠️.
- **Kirsch distilling:** first mentioned in **1626**, as a *ban* on distilling «Kriesywasser». ✅
- **Chriesigloggä (cherry bell):** the «Grosse Glocke» is first named in the city council minutes on **4 July 1711**, and the «Erlaubnis-Glocke» (permission bell) on **23 June 1769**. ✅
- **Chriesisturm (the rush to the trees):** the old custom is known from **one retelling in the «Zuger Zeitung», 1886**. ✅ The **ladder race through the Old Town was invented in 2009** by Ueli Kleeb. It is a modern creation inspired by that article, not a custom that survived unbroken. ✅ This is the most important nuance for the story (see §6).
- **Zuger Kirschtorte:** made by Heinrich (Heiri) Höhn. The first advertisement appeared in **1915**. ✅
- **Modern revival:** the IG Zuger Chriesi was founded in 2008, the bell has rung again since 2008, the race has run since 2009, the cherry-growing culture was listed as a «Lebendige Tradition der Schweiz» in 2011, Zuger Kirsch became AOP in 2013, the Kirschtorte became IGP in 2015, and the 1000th new cherry tree was planted in 2018.

## 2. Where the cherry comes from (before Zug)

| When | What | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Prehistory | The wild sweet cherry (*Prunus avium*, also called Vogelkirsche or mazzard) is native to Europe and western Asia. Its wild form grew north of the Alps long before anyone farmed it. Domesticated cherries probably come from the Black Sea and Caspian region. Genome studies show a clear split between wild and cultivated trees. | ✅ |
| c. 1060–880 BC | **Zug-Sumpf**, a Late Bronze Age lakeshore village between Chollermüli (Zug) and Alpenblick (Cham). It was discovered in 1859 while the Zug–Lucerne railway was being built. Plant-remains studies (Neuweiler 1931; Jacomet et al. 1989) list *Prunus avium* among the potentially useful plants, **based on a single stone**. That makes it a wild cherry someone gathered, not a cultivated one. | ✅ (one find only) |
| 74 BC (according to Pliny) | Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 15.102) writes that Lucullus brought the cherry to Italy from Pontus after defeating Mithridates, and that it reached Britain within 120 years. Athenaeus names the town of **Kerasos** (today Giresun, Turkey). **Modern scholars doubt this.** Kołoczek (2022) shows that Lucullus cannot have been the first, and that Pliny's dates fit his Rome-centred worldview. | ⚠️ Treat as legend |
| Roman period | The Romans spread grafted sweet cherries across the Alps. Stone finds from Roman sites support this. | ✅ |
| **AD 170–270** | **Cham-Hagendorn (Canton Zug):** the excavation of 1944/45, with follow-up digs in 2003/04, uncovered one of the best-preserved Roman water mills in Europe, a smithy and a sanctuary. The finds include **stones of sweet and sour cherries**, radiocarbon-dated to 170–270 AD. Key publication: Schucany & Winet, *Schmiede – Heiligtum – Wassermühle*, Antiqua 52 (2014). | ✅ |

**The word.** *Chriesi* comes from Old High German *chirsa* and Middle High German *kirse*, which go back to Vulgar Latin *ceresia*, Latin *cerasus* and Greek *kérasos* (sweet cherry tree). The Greek word probably comes from a pre-Greek or Anatolian language. Popular accounts say it is named after the town of Kerasos, but that link is disputed. The Swiss German *ch-* is the regular sound change (compare *Chind* for "Kind"). ✅ for the chain of word forms, ⚠️ for the Kerasos link.

## 3. Zug's cherry history, step by step

### Middle Ages and early modern period
- **Mid-14th century:** first written mentions of cherry growing in the Zug area. ✅
- The cherry trees on the **Zuger Allmend** (common land) belonged to the city's citizens jointly. Cherry *wood* was valuable too, and people who cut it illegally (*Holzfrevler*) were fined or jailed. ✅
- **1626:** the first record of distilling in the city of Zug is a **ban on distilling «Kriesywasser»**. Cherries were apparently being turned into spirits on a scale that worried the authorities. ✅
- **1627:** first mention of the **Zuger Chriesimärt** on today's Landsgemeindeplatz. ✅

### 18th century: bell, guards, rules
- **4 July 1711:** the city council minutes name the «Grosse Glocke» of **St. Michael** in connection with the cherry harvest. This is the earliest record of the Chriesigloggä. ✅
- **1731:** **Baar** has its own cherry bell at the parish church of **St. Martin**. ✅ Today both bells ring at noon on the same day.
- **1751:** people from Baar reportedly picked cherries on the *Zug* Allmend, a sign of rivalry between neighbours. ⚠️ (mentioned briefly on stadtzug.ch, no details)
- **23 June 1769:** the minutes mention the «Erlaubnis-Glocke» (permission bell). ✅
- How it worked, according to the city of Zug and the 1886 retelling:
  - Picking before the bell was **strictly forbidden**.
  - The city hired **Chriesiwächter** (cherry guards) who watched the trees day and night in the weeks before the harvest.
  - Thieves were fined or locked in the **Zytturm** (clock tower) prison.
  - At **12 noon**, the largest bell of St. Michael rang **for a quarter of an hour**. Then every Zug citizen could rush to the Allmend with **ladders and baskets**.
  - **The first person to lean a ladder against a tree could pick it.**
  - The cry: «D'Chriäsigloggä lüütet, dä Chriäsisturm god lous!»
- 18th-century kitchen: the **«Türkenbund»**, also called the Chriesiauflauf, was a baked dish of cherries, eggs, butter and durum semolina, similar to a French clafoutis. Its recipe survives in an old cookbook in the Baar archives and is called the oldest surviving Zug cherry recipe. ✅ (swissinfo, Helvetic Kitchen)
- **Kirsch** already had a good reputation beyond Switzerland in the 18th century. ✅

### Why the old custom ended
- In the second half of the 18th century, almost all common land in Canton Zug was **divided up and privatised** (Historisches Lexikon der Schweiz). Once the cherry trees became private property, a public rush to pick them no longer made sense. ✅
- The IG Zuger Chriesi says the custom faded in the 19th century and the Allmend trees were neglected around 1800. ⚠️
- 1886: the custom was already history. The «Zuger Zeitung» printed a **retelling** of it, which suggests readers no longer knew it first-hand. ✅

### 19th century: Kirsch becomes an export
- **1798:** the Bosshard distillery opens in Zug's Old Town. ✅
- **1846:** the Fassbind distillery opens in Arth (Canton Schwyz). From the mid-19th century, Zug–Rigi Kirschwasser is an important **export product**. ✅
- In the second half of the 19th century, Switzerland had more fruit trees per person than any other country in Europe (HLS). ✅
- **1870:** cherry farmers and distillers found the **Kirschwasser-Gesellschaft in Zug** to raise quality and stop fakes. ✅
- **1872:** the society builds premises at Chamerstrasse 6, with storage for 500 tonnes of cherries and **steam stills by Carl Georg Siemens** (Stuttgart). Chemistry professor F. C. Mühlberg acts as independent quality controller. ✅
- Joseph Schmidt's steam distillery wins awards in Santiago de Chile (1875) and Paris (1879), and advertises 24 medals in 1901. ✅
- **1914:** the Kirschwasser-Gesellschaft wins gold at the Swiss National Exhibition in Bern. It closes in **1932**. Its successor, KIWAG, is based at Etter Söhne AG in Zug. ✅
- **1932:** the canton has **577 distilleries**, 42 of them large-scale. ✅

### 20th century: the cake, then decline
- **1913:** confectioner **Heinrich (Heiri) Höhn** (1889–1957) takes over an inn in Zug and opens a small confectionery. ✅
- **1915:** the first advertisement for a «Zuger Kirschtorte» appears. Sources disagree on the newspaper (Zuger Nachrichten or Zuger Zeitung). ✅ for the year, ⚠️ for the paper.
- **1923:** the cake wins a gold medal. ✅ (Wikipedia; not independently checked)
- **1943:** Höhn hands the business to his head confectioner **Jacques Treichler**. The Konditorei Treichler now runs a Kirschtorten museum with over 200 objects. ✅
- **1951:** the cherry-tree count peaks at **44,482** (about 44,500) standard trees in the canton. ✅
- After that, building, changes in farming and low cherry prices cause a steep fall: **16,500 trees in 2001** across the canton. Around the city, the count fell from about **4,000 in 1960 to about 1,500**. ✅
- The bell custom was forgotten in the 20th century. ✅

### 21st century: «Chriesi-Aktivismus» and the revival
- **2006:** Ueli Kleeb (graphic designer and artist) and Heiri Scherer launch the idea **«1000 Kirschbäume für Zug»**. ✅
- **2008:** the **IG Zuger Chriesi** is founded. On **30 June 2008** the Chriesigloggä rings again for the first time in many years. ✅ The Zuger Kirschtorte joins the inventory of Swiss culinary heritage. ✅
- **2009:** the **Chriesisturm ladder race** through the Old Town is created (see §6). Zug, Schwyz and Lucerne found the association Zuger & Rigi Chriesi. The **Chriesiwurst** (sausage with dried cherries) goes on sale. ✅
- **2010:** a **Kindersturm** (children's race) is added. ✅ The cantonal government and city council make cherries part of their legislative goals in 2010–11. ✅
- **2011:** cherry growing in Canton Zug is added to the **«Lebendige Traditionen der Schweiz»** list (one source says 2010). ⚠️ for the year. The **Chriesigloggä** chocolate bell with cherry jelly goes on sale. ✅
- **2012:** **Chriesibier** from a brewery in Baar. **2013:** **Chriesijoghurt** (Nestlé Hirz), and **Zuger Kirsch AOP / Rigi Kirsch AOP** protection. ✅
- **2015:** the **Zuger Kirschtorte** becomes IGP: only cakes made in Canton Zug may use the name. ✅
- **2017:** Ueli Kleeb and Caroline Lötscher (eds.) publish **«CHRIESI – Kirschenkultur rund um Zugersee und Rigi»**, the standard reference (Swiss Print Award 2019). ✅
- **2018:** a **Huttenrennen** (women's race with back baskets) is added. ⚠️ In **December 2018** the **1000th tree** is planted, completing 1000 trees at 80 sites around Zug. ✅
- **2020:** the race is cancelled because of the pandemic. The bell still rings on 22 June. ✅
- **2023:** a Matura thesis by **Sina Meyer** (Baar) shows that the race only dates from 2009 (see §6). ✅
- **2026:** the bell is announced for **Monday, 22 June 2026**. The 2027 date is still open; it depends on when the cherries ripen. ⚠️

## 4. The customs today

| Custom | What happens | Since |
|---|---|---|
| **Chriesigloggä** | Monday at 12:00, mid to late June, depending on ripeness. St. Michael in Zug and St. Martin in Baar ring at the same time. | Recorded 1711 / 1731, revived 2008 |
| **Chriesisturm** | Starts at the Liebfrauenkapelle, on the first stroke of the bell. Men's teams of two carry **8 m wooden ladders**, school teams carry **4 m** ladders, and women run with **Hutten** (back baskets). The race lasts about 15 minutes, with a few hundred spectators. The winners get honour and applause. The losing team gives up its starting place the following year. Afterwards there is a party on the Landsgemeindeplatz with Chriesiwurst and Kirschtorte. The ladders hang on the walls of the old Rathaus all year. | 2009 |
| **Chriesimärt** | From 15:00 on bell day on the Landsgemeindeplatz, then for 2–3 weeks, Monday to Friday, 15:00–18:00. Local growers sell fresh cherries and cherry products. | Recorded 1627 |
| Others | Chriesi-Tour (an audio tour with 14 stories), the cherry blossom hike from Zug to Goldau. | Recent |

**Varieties.** Switzerland has about 350 cherry varieties, and so does the Zug–Rigi region. **63 distilling varieties** are officially defined for the Kirsch region. A modern table cherry named in sources is «Kordia». For the Hochstamm (standard-tree) varieties, the Kleeb/Lötscher book is the reference.

## 5. «Ins Glas»: what the cherries turned into

1. **Fresh**, sold at the Chriesimärt (since at least 1627).
2. **Dried (Dörrchriesi):** before canning existed, drying was, along with cool cellar storage, the most important way to preserve fruit in Switzerland. Households dried fruit in the tiled stove, and larger farms built drying kilns, often next to the distilling house. Dried fruit was traded inside Switzerland and exported. ✅ in general; I found no Zug-specific source.
3. **Kirsch / Chriesiwasser:** the historically dominant product (1626 ban, 18th-century reputation, 19th-century exports, AOP since 2013).
   - AOP rules: only cherries harvested in the Zug–Rigi region, distilled there, aged at least 6 months.
   - The region covers all of Canton Zug, 9 Schwyz municipalities (including Arth, Küssnacht, Schwyz and Gersau) and 7 Lucerne municipalities (including Weggis, Meggen and Vitznau).
   - Output is about 50,000 litres a year, of which about 15,000 litres go into cakes. Sold in more than 20 countries.
4. **Cooked and baked:** the 18th-century Türkenbund/Chriesiauflauf; the Kirschtorte from 1915; jam and preserved cherries in jars.
   - ⚠️ I found **no Zug-specific historical source for jam-making**. Home preserving in glass jars was common in Switzerland in the 20th century, but nothing documents it as a special Zug custom.
   - If the story's «Glas» means jam jars, that is a reasonable, child-friendly choice, but it should not be presented as the historic Zug product. Historically, the cherries went into the **bottle** (Kirsch).

## 6. Things the project should get right

1. **Don't call the ladder race an ancient custom.** The present-day Chriesisturm race dates from 2009.
   - What is historical: the bell (1711), the ban and the guards, and the free-for-all rush to the Allmend with ladders and baskets (1886 retelling).
   - Our narration line «Die Läuferinnen und Läufer rennen mit ihren langen Holzleitern los» describes today's race, which is fine because we present it as today's event.
   - If any text says something like «seit 300 Jahren rennen die Zuger…», change it to «Schon vor 300 Jahren läutete die Chriesiglogge…».
2. **Exact bell wording:** «Die Chriesigloggä der Kirche St. Michael» (and St. Martin in Baar). The historical bell rang **at noon for a quarter of an hour**. Our story has the child tap the bell to start the race, which matches.
3. **«UNESCO» is overstated.** Cherry growing in Zug is on the *Swiss* list of living traditions, which is drawn up under the UNESCO convention. It is **not** on UNESCO's own world list. Several tourism sites get this wrong. Safe wording: «gehört seit 2011 zu den lebendigen Traditionen der Schweiz».
4. **«Ausgegraben» works well with real archaeology.** The intro has the villager dig up a chest with a book and a cherry stone, and Zug really does have dug-up cherry stones:
   - the Roman stones from **Cham-Hagendorn** (AD 170–270), found next to a water mill;
   - the single wild-cherry stone from the Bronze Age village of **Zug-Sumpf** (about 3,000 years old).
   - A line such as «Schon die Römer haben hier Chriesi gegessen. Archäologen haben ihre Steine in Cham ausgegraben!» is accurate and age-appropriate. This needs approval with Kinderkultur Zug (SW04).
5. **The Lucullus story** («a Roman general brought the cherry to Europe») is a legend. Don't present it as fact. The wild cherry was already native here.
6. **Bird:** the print spread shows a **blackbird** (Amsel) on the ladder, while the 3D scene and narration use a **starling** (Star). Both eat cherries, but the print and the AR scene should probably match.
7. **Ladder lengths:** if the 3D ladders are meant to look like the real race, they are 8 m for adults and 4 m for school teams.

## 7. Open questions / where to dig further

- The **Stadtarchiv Zug** holds the 1711 and 1769 council minutes, and the **«Zuger Zeitung» 1886** has the original retelling. These are worth getting as primary sources if the magazine wants a quote.
- **Kleeb & Lötscher, «CHRIESI»** (2017) is the most complete secondary source; see chriesi.ch.
- **Sina Meyer's Matura thesis** (2023, Baar, supervised by Jonas Briner) on how the race was invented.
- **Schucany & Winet 2014** (Antiqua 52) for the exact archaeobotanical details on the Hagendorn cherry stones.
- The Historisches Lexikon der Schweiz articles «Zug (Gemeinde)», «Obstbau» and «Allmend» for citable background.
- The **official 2027 bell date**, from the IG Zuger Chriesi or Zug Tourismus. The magazine text says «Im Juni» and should not give a fixed date.
